Observation and Analysis

LSU-ITAC observed that in the four buildings area, there were eight 5-ton cooling units in operation with a total capacity of 40 tons, set at an average temperature of 72°F. These cooling units were controlled by eight programmable thermostats. According to the management team, the thermostats were kept at a constant temperature. Such control strategies will result in unnecessary energy usage by the cooling unit, since the real-time temperature may still be higher than the set points during the off-hours and weekends. If the facility installs the programmable thermostats (see Figure 4-1) in place of the non-programmable thermostats and sets up a temperature schedule on the existing programmable thermostats for the cooling units, there will be energy savings coming from the reduced working hours of the cooling units that otherwise would be working.

Figure 4-1. The Types of Programmable Thermostats.

Recommendation

We recommend that the facility set up a temperature schedule on the existing programmable thermostats. The thermostats should be programmed such that when the office areas are unoccupied, the setpoints should be raised to 80°F, and revert to their current setpoints an hour before the shift begins. When the indoor temperature is below the setpoint, the cooling units will stop operating. By doing so, the total cooling hours and load will be reduced, resulting in noticeable energy and cost savings.

Calculations

Current Electrical Energy Usage

We assume that the indoor temperature equals the outdoor temperature over a year. The current cooling hours were estimated using TMY3 data specific to the local area, using temperature-bin analysis. The hours' count for the cooling unit when the indoor air temperature is higher than the setpoints and requires cooling over a year is shown in Figure 4-2. The row levels represent the temperature range (°F), and column labels represent the time range of day (hour) for a 24-hour clock period.

Figure 4-2. The Cooling Hour Count for the Cooling Unit Set at 72°F

From the hour count, we know that there generally have been 3,461 hours in a year when the outside temperature was higher than 72°F. Thus, we can calculate the current cooling energy usage of the cooling units as follows:

EUCC = (RCT × CH72 × LFC / COPC) × KT

= ((40 ton) × 3,461 hrs/yr × 1.0 / 3.28) × 3.5 kW/ton

= 147,726 kWh/yr,

where

EUCC = Current Cooling Energy Usage, kWh/yr

RCT = Total Rate Capacity of Cooling Units, tons

CH72 = Total Cooling Hours in a Year Based on 72°F

LFC = Estimated Load Factor for Cooling Units, estimated at 1.0

COPC = Cooling Coefficient of Performance, estimated at 3.28

KT = Conversion Factor, 3.5 kW/ton

Proposed Electrical Energy Usage

It is recommended that the cooling units start cooling the office an hour before space begins to be occupied. Choosing the time range to show the cooling hours based on 72 – 80°F that fall into shift hours, we change the pivot table, as shown in Figure 4-3.

Figure 4-3. The Cooling Hours Count Based on 72 – 80°F in a Proposed Schedule

Since there are six workdays in a seven-day week, 1/7 of the 482 cooling hours based on 72 – 80°F that fall into shift hours can be reduced as it represents non-operational days. Therefore, we estimate that the cooling hours during working hours are as:

CHW = 482 × (6/7) + 152 + 1,348 + 545

= 2,458 hrs/yr,

where

CHW = Cooling Hours during the Working Hours under Proposed Schedule, hrs/yr

CHT = Total Cooling Hours during Working Hours with Weekends included under the Proposed Schedule, hrs/yr

This new setpoint schedule will result in 2,458 annual cooling hours for the office. The proposed cooling setpoints schedule is summarized in Table 4-2.

= ((40 -ton) × 2,458 hrs/yr × 1.0 / 3.28) × 3.5 kW/ton

Electrical Energy Savings

The electrical energy savings can be calculated:

ES = EUCC – EUCP

= 147,726 kWh/yr – 104,915 kWh/yr

= 42,811 kWh/yr,

where

ES = Electrical Energy Savings from Cooling, kWh/yr

EUCC = Current Cooling Energy Usage, kWh/yr

EUCP = Proposed Cooling Energy Usage, kWh/yr

Electrical Energy Cost Savings

The electrical energy cost savings can be calculated:

ECS = ES × UR

= 42,811 kWh/yr × $0.082/kWh

= $3,511/yr,

where

ECS = Electrical Energy Cost Savings from Cooling, $/yr

ES = Electrical Energy Savings from Cooling, kWh/yr

UR = Average Electrical Energy Usage Rate, $0.082/kWh

Total Energy Cost Savings

In this case, the total cost savings are equal to the electrical energy cost savings:

TCS = ECS

= $3,511/yr,

where

TCS = Total Cost Savings, $/yr

ECS = Electrical Energy Cost Savings, $/yr

Carbon Dioxide Reduction

Due to the reduced electrical usage, there will also be lower carbon dioxide emissions at the electric power generation. These reductions can be calculated using:

CO2 = ES × K1 × K2

= 42,811 kWh/yr × (0.763 lb. CO2 / kWh) × 1Ton/2,000lbs

= 16 Tons/yr,

where

CO2 = Amount of Carbon Dioxide Reduced, Tons CO2/yr

K1 = Amount of CO2 Produced per kWh, 0.763 lb. CO2/kWh

K2 = Conversion Factor = 1Ton/2,000lbs

Implementation Cost

The office had eight programmable thermostats. Implementing a new temperature schedule will involve labor costs for an employee to set up all the thermostats, without any material costs. Assuming the labor cost is $25/hr, and the installation and setup time is 0.2 hour per thermostat, the total time required will be (8 × 0.2 hour) = 1.6 hour of labor.
